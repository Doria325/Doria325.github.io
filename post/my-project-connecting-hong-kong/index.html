My Project: Connecting Hong Kong

2026-07-24

A Brief History & Why the MTR was Built

HK had terrible traffic due to the explosive population growth. It went from 600,000 people to 3.7 million just within 20 years after the Japanese occupation. This leads to a drastic increase in travel demand.

But congestion was not a simple problem that can be solved due to the special geographic location of this place. It’s not just surrounded by water, but also most of the land is hilly, which makes it difficult to develop. 40% of the land was also protected by the government as country park, further restricting land development and land supply. While newcomers flood in the narrow strips of land, the city also couldn’t expand outwards, giving even more pressure to the existing roads. In 1967, 75% of the transportation was already on the surface with buses and trams, so adding more surface transportation service will create more congestion. Meanwhile, the rise of the use of private vehicles made roads even crowder.

In the Hong Kong Mass Transport Study from the 1960s, the report concluded three main problems with the Hong Kong transportation system. 1. Surface transport alone is inadequate. 2. Limited road mileage necessitated more efficient public transport. 3. A new metro is needed.

When planning the MTR as a public sector business, the government hoped that it could maintain financial sustainability. But for public transportation, breaking even alone is already a challenge, let alone turn a profit just by offering transit services. A funding gap between revenues and costs also encouraged the government to enter other business activities, such as charging telcos to offer cell service underground, building larger stations to accommodate stores, and even developing properties around the stations–which turned out to be a very brilliant strategy.

Metro+Property & How it Works:

Property makes up a huge portion of MTR’s profit. This includes HK’s two tallest office towers–International Commerce Centre (ICC) and Two International Finance Centre (Two IFC); residential developments like The Riverpark, The Austin; malls like The Wai, Maritime Square. Here’s how the system exactly works:

First, the HK government grants land development rights to MTR. Then, MTR planners distribute the lands to several different development packages for different uses to development partners. The MTR must pay a premium to the government that reflects the land price set at “before rail” price, which assumes the MTR is not there yet. But the land developers pay at a higher price to the MTR raised by the development of the metro. MTR gets to turn this difference into profit.

HK MTR Map in 1989

By 1985, 40% of homes and 50% of workspaces were within a 10 minute walk distance from a MTR station.

Building on this model, the metro that runs from the city to the new airport follows the same planning concept. The railway created new neighborhoods, like Tung Chung, which used to be a fishing village. In 2012, the Tung Chung New Town Extension Study was commissioned by the government with the overall objective of extending it to a distinct community that meets housing, social, economic, local and environmental needs. It accommodated the lives of up to 100,000 people in 2023, acting like an independent community with a mall, residential buildings, office areas, that supports the operation of the airport. This was especially convenient for people working in or related to the airport. However, a current issue is that the area has expanded to an extent where some residential developments are already outside the range of the metro station. But overall in this case, we see how MTR acted as a guideline for locating new housing, employment and commercial activities rather than generating transportation services for the existing population. It strengthened the connection between infrastructure planning and land-use development.

More than just Metro Station

The first railway property, Telford Gardens, is a housing estate that sits directly above the MTR Kowloon Bay Depot and Kowloon Bay Station. It directly connects with the Telford Plaza, a shopping mall that gives residents access to exclusive sports facilities, retail shops and dining options. This model that connects transportation directly with residence area and commercial area was a common combination in the MTR planning system in HK. MTR stations are directly inside shopping malls, hotels and office buildings. A perfect blend of the different spaces prevents you from noticing that you have entered a metro station from a separate environment, and vice versa. This is due to a consistent interior design and services: as previously mentioned, stations have stores, restaurants to generate additional revenue streams. Fully covered, airconed footbridges and underpasses that reach further areas. Later, we see this model as an inspiration for metro stations in mainland China and even in other countries like Singapore.

By deliberately planning them in a way that connects spaces, the station network transforms into a second street underground. I’ve tried walking from my hotel at the more northern Tsim Sha Tsui to K11 MUSEA at the south. On the map, the estimated time needed for this walk is approximately 10 minutes, but the actual time required can be up to 15-18 minutes due to the crowded and narrow street. But if I walk through the closest metro stations, the time required to get to the destination would only be around 8 minutes. In Hong Kong, you can almost always find a path underground that takes you to your destination in a much shorter time compared to walking on the surface. This is extremely important in making daily commute much convenient in such a crowded, busy city.
